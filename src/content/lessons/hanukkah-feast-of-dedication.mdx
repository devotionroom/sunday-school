> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A note for the teacher — historical accuracy matters here**
>
> Hanukkah is a Jewish holiday with a rich and carefully documented history. Two things are important to get right:
>
> **1. The 8-day celebration comes from the rededication itself**, as described in 1 Maccabees 4:36–59 and 2 Maccabees 10:1–8. The Maccabees found that they had missed the festival of Sukkot while fighting, so they celebrated the rededication for 8 days "as at Sukkot." This is the biblical and historical basis for Hanukkah.
>
> **2. The "miracle of the oil" is a later Jewish tradition**, found in the Babylonian Talmud (tractate Shabbat 21b), written several centuries after the events. It is not in the biblical or deuterocanonical books of Maccabees. Children may have heard this tradition — it is widely known and cherished in Jewish culture. Present it honestly: *"There is a beloved Jewish tradition about a small flask of oil that lasted 8 days. That tradition comes from the Talmud, written centuries later — it is not in the original Maccabees account. But the tradition points to something real: God's miraculous faithfulness to His people."* Do not dismiss it; simply distinguish it from the historical record.
>
> **Respect the Jewish origins of this holiday.** Present Hanukkah as what it is: a celebration of God's faithfulness in preserving His people and their Temple worship. The connection to Jesus as the Light of the World is a beautiful and genuine theological layer — but it does not replace the Jewish story.

## The Bible Story

### 1. The Temple Desecrated

The Temple in Jerusalem was the most sacred place in the world for the Jewish people. It was where the priests offered sacrifices to God, where God's presence dwelt, and where the people gathered to worship Him.

Around 167 BC, a Greek king named **Antiochus IV Epiphanes** seized the Temple. He desecrated it — he placed an idol in the most holy place and stopped the daily sacrifices. The worship of God was forbidden. Jewish people who kept God's laws were persecuted.

Imagine a place your family loved — a place that was holy and special — and someone came and ruined it. That is what this felt like to the Jewish people.

The light had gone out.

**Teaching Point:** God's people were not abandoned in the darkness. Even when it seemed like everything was lost, God was not finished.

### 2. The Maccabean Revolt

A Jewish priest named **Mattathias** and his sons — especially **Judah Maccabee** — refused to bow to Antiochus' idols. They led a revolt against the Greek forces.

It was not easy. They were outnumbered. They were not a professional army. But they were fighting for the right to worship God freely, and they trusted that God was with them.

After years of fighting, in **164 BC**, the Maccabees drove Antiochus' forces out of Jerusalem and took back the Temple.

*Ask: "Have you ever had to stand up for something that was right, even when it was hard?"*

**Teaching Point:** God used an unlikely family of priests to preserve the worship of His people. Obedience to God sometimes requires great courage.

### 3. The Rededication

The Maccabees cleaned and purified the Temple. They rebuilt the altar. They restored the sacred items.

Then they rededicated the Temple — they gave it back to God. And they celebrated for **8 days**, like the great 8-day festival of Sukkot they had missed while fighting. They offered sacrifices again. They worshipped God again.

The account in 1 Maccabees 4:59 says:
> *"Then Judah and his brothers and all the assembly of Israel determined that every year at that season the days of dedication of the altar should be observed with joy and gladness for eight days."*

This is the origin of **Hanukkah** — the Hebrew word for *dedication*. The 8-day celebration of the restored worship of God.

> **A note about the oil tradition:** Many children may have heard a story that a small flask of oil miraculously lasted 8 days. This beloved tradition comes from the Talmud, written centuries after the Maccabees. The original Maccabees account does not mention this miracle. We can honour the tradition while being honest that the 8-day celebration was established first because of the rededication itself — which is itself a remarkable story of God's faithfulness.

**Teaching Point:** The worship of God was restored. This is worth celebrating — and the Jewish people have celebrated it every year since.

### 4. The Feast of Dedication in John

Centuries later, Jesus walked in Jerusalem during this very festival.

John 10:22–23 tells us:
> *"Then came the Feast of Dedication at Jerusalem. It was winter, and Jesus was in the temple area walking in Solomon's Colonnade."*

Jesus was there. He stood in the Temple courts that the Maccabees had reclaimed and rededicated. He walked where the priests had restored the worship of God.

This is a small but significant detail. Jesus did not avoid Hanukkah. He was present at it.

*Ask: "Why do you think it matters that Jesus was there at the Feast of Dedication?"*

**Teaching Point:** Jesus did not come to erase Jewish history. He stepped into it. He stood in the very place where God's faithfulness had been celebrated for generations.

### 5. Jesus the Light of the World

Earlier in John's Gospel — in John 8:12 — Jesus had made a declaration that connects powerfully to Hanukkah and its celebration of light:

> **"I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will never walk in darkness but will have the light of life."** — John 8:12 (BSB)

Hanukkah is called the Festival of Lights. The Temple lights were restored. The worship of God was restored. And into this very context, Jesus says: *I am the Light.*

He is not simply a lamp in a building. He is the Light of the whole world — the true Light that the Temple's restored lamps were pointing toward.

**Teaching Point:** The Hanukkah lights point forward to Jesus, the true and everlasting Light of the World. When we follow Him, we are never left in darkness.

## The Deeper Meaning

### 1. God Preserves His People and His Worship
The story of Hanukkah is a story of God's faithfulness. When it looked like the worship of God was finished — when the Temple was desecrated and the people were scattered — God preserved them. He raised up unlikely people to do what seemed impossible. He restored what had been taken.

We can trust that the same God who preserved His people then is the same God who is at work in our lives today.

### 2. Light Matters Most in the Darkness
One light in a dark room changes everything. The Jewish people had experienced the darkness of oppression. When the Temple was rededicated and the light was restored, it was not just symbolic — it was real and profound. Light matters most when you have been in the dark.

Jesus said He is the light of the world. That promise means most to those who have known what darkness feels like.

### 3. Jesus Is the True Light the Festival Points To
Hanukkah points to something greater than restored oil lamps. When Jesus stood at the Feast of Dedication and declared He was the Light of the World, He was connecting the dots. The Temple lights were restored. But He is the light that cannot be extinguished — the light of life itself.

## Age Adaptations

<AgeGroup level="preschool">
### Younger Children
For younger children, simplify the lesson to three ideas:
- **The Temple went dark.** A mean king stopped the people from worshipping God.
- **The Temple was fixed.** Some brave people cleaned the Temple and gave it back to God. Everyone celebrated for 8 days!
- **Jesus is the Light.** Jesus said, "I am the light of the world." Even one small light pushes the dark away.

**Simple Question:** *"Who is the light of the world?"*
**Answer:** Jesus!

**Simple Take-Home Truth:** *"Jesus is the light — and when I follow Him, I am never in the dark."*

For younger children, use the Light in the Darkness activity as the centrepiece. The flashlight or battery-operated candle will do more than a long explanation. Keep the historical content brief.
</AgeGroup>

<AgeGroup level="upper-elementary">
### Older Children
Older children can engage with the historical complexity and theological depth of Hanukkah.

Ask:
- Why do you think it mattered so much to the Jewish people that the Temple was rededicated — and not just rebuilt?
- What is the difference between the "miracle of the oil" tradition and the historical account in Maccabees? Why does it matter to be accurate?
- John 10:22–23 is a small detail — Jesus was at Hanukkah. Why might John include that detail?
- If Jesus is the "light of the world," what are some forms of darkness He pushes back?
- How does Hanukkah help us understand Jesus more deeply, without replacing the Jewish meaning of the holiday?

**Challenge:**
Have students read John 10:22–30 and ask: What does Jesus say about Himself in those verses? What do the people ask Him? What does He claim?

Emphasize that Hanukkah is a Jewish holiday with a profound history — and that Christians who understand it gain a richer picture of the world Jesus walked into and what He claimed to be within it.
</AgeGroup>

9. How does Jesus' declaration "I am the light of the world" connect to the Hanukkah story?

11. What does it mean for Jesus to be a light in that kind of darkness?
12. If you wanted to follow the light Jesus offers, what would that look like in your daily life?
13. How can you respect and appreciate the Jewish origins of Hanukkah while also seeing how it connects to Jesus?

## Memory Verse

**John 8:12 (BSB)**
> **"I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will never walk in darkness but will have the light of life."**

### Teach the Meaning
Ask: *"What do you think Jesus means when He calls Himself the light of the world?"*

Explain: Light reveals what is hidden in the darkness. Light guides us so we don't stumble. Light is life — plants, animals, people all need light to live. Jesus is saying that He does all of those things for the soul. He reveals truth. He guides us. He gives life that lasts forever.

### Teaching Method

Darken the room as much as possible. Ask children to think about what it feels like to be in the dark. Light a single battery-operated candle. Show how even one small light changes everything.

Read the verse aloud once.

Then teach phrase by phrase:

> **"I am the light of the world."**
*(Children repeat — hold up hands like a candle flame)*

> **"Whoever follows me"**
*(Children repeat — take one step forward)*

> **"will never walk in darkness"**
*(Children repeat — shade eyes as if looking through darkness)*

> **"but will have the light of life."**
*(Children repeat — open hands wide like light spreading)*

For younger children, focus on the first phrase: **"I am the light of the world."** Add a simple motion — both hands raised like a candle flame above the head.

## Application

Ask: **What is one dark place — in your life, in the world — where you need Jesus' light right now?**

Give children time to think quietly.

Then ask: **What would it look like to follow the light instead of staying in the dark?**

Remind them: The Maccabees did not sit still in the darkness. They did something — they fought to restore the light. God calls us to walk in the light too.

When darkness says: *"This is hopeless."*
We can remember: **"Jesus is the light of the world."**

When we feel lost or confused:
We can remember: **"Whoever follows me will never walk in darkness."**

Then ask: **"What is one step I can take this week to follow the light?"**

## Take-Home Challenge

This week, when something feels dark — confusing, scary, or sad:

### PAUSE
Stop and take a breath.

### REMEMBER
Jesus said: *"I am the light of the world."* He has not changed.

### TURN TOWARD THE LIGHT
Tell Jesus what feels dark. Ask Him to shine His light into it.

### TAKE ONE STEP FORWARD
Following the light means moving — even a small step. What is one right thing you can do?

A simple prayer:
> **"Jesus, You are the light of the world. Please shine Your light in [this situation]. Help me to follow You and not stay in the dark. Amen."**

## Closing Thought

Hanukkah has been celebrated for over two thousand years.

Every year, Jewish families around the world light the menorah candles and remember the rededication of the Temple — the moment when the light was restored, when the worship of God was given back to His people.

That story is a true story of God's faithfulness. God did not abandon His people. God did not let the darkness win.

And Jesus, standing in those very Temple courts during that very festival, looked at the world and said: *"I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will never walk in darkness."*

The Hanukkah candles point to something real: God preserves His people. The light is not defeated. And in Jesus, we have the light of life — a light no darkness can overcome.
